Unit 1 · Foundations

The C*-Identity as a Geometry of Operations

This first challenge set introduces C*-algebras not merely as algebraic gadgets, but as spaces where multiplication, adjoint, norm, positivity, and spectrum conspire to form a rigid analytic geometry. The guiding question is: what kind of mathematical world is forced into existence by the identity \(\|a^*a\|=\|a\|^2\)?

LevelAdvanced undergraduate / beginning graduate
FormatSeminar through problems
PrerequisitesLinear algebra, normed spaces, basic complex analysis helpful
ThemeAlgebra + topology + spectrum

Learning Goals

By the end of this unit, the student should begin to see why C*-algebras are simultaneously algebraic, analytic, spectral, and geometric objects.

Recognize the structure.

Identify the roles played by multiplication, involution, norm, completeness, and the C*-identity.

Understand rigidity.

See how the C*-identity strongly constrains the norm and ties algebraic operations to geometry.

Connect to models.

Relate abstract examples to concrete operator algebras and commutative function algebras.

Problem Set 1

Work slowly. The problems are ordered so that earlier questions give conceptual handles for later ones. Hints and discussion notes are included, but the intended rhythm is to attempt each problem before opening them.

1

Normed *-Algebras: The Basic Grammar

Definitions · Examples · Counterexamples

A normed *-algebra over \(\mathbb C\) is an algebra \(A\) equipped with a norm \(\|\cdot\|\) and an involution \(a \mapsto a^*\) satisfying

\[ (a+b)^*=a^*+b^*,\qquad (\lambda a)^*=\overline{\lambda}a^*,\qquad (ab)^*=b^*a^*,\qquad (a^*)^*=a. \]
  1. Explain why the formula \((ab)^*=b^*a^*\), rather than \((ab)^*=a^*b^*\), is the natural one for matrices.
  2. Show that \(M_n(\mathbb C)\), with conjugate-transpose as involution, is a *-algebra.
  3. Give an example of a commutative *-algebra and explain why the order reversal in \((ab)^*=b^*a^*\) becomes invisible there.
  4. Explain why completeness is not yet part of the definition of a normed *-algebra.
Hint
Think of matrices as linear transformations. The adjoint is defined by the inner product identity \(\langle Tv,w\rangle=\langle v,T^*w\rangle\). Apply this twice to \(ST\).
Discussion
The involution is not merely decoration. It encodes a reversal of operational order: if \(ab\) means “first apply \(b\), then apply \(a\),” the adjoint reverses the chain of action.

The C*-Identity as a Norm Constraint

Core Identity

A C*-algebra is a Banach *-algebra \(A\) satisfying the identity

\[\|a^*a\|=\|a\|^2 \qquad \text{for every } a\in A.\]
  1. Assuming the norm is submultiplicative, show that \(\|a^*a\|\leq \|a^*\|\|a\|\).
  2. Use the C*-identity to explain why the involution cannot be wildly discontinuous.
  3. In a C*-algebra, prove that \(\|a^*\|=\|a\|\).
  4. Explain conceptually why this makes the involution an isometry.
Hint
Apply the C*-identity to \(a\) and then to \(a^*\). Remember that \((a^*)^*a^*=aa^*\).
Solution Sketch
From submultiplicativity, \(\|a\|^2=\|a^*a\|\leq \|a^*\|\|a\|\), so for \(a\neq 0\), \(\|a\|\leq \|a^*\|\). Applying the same argument to \(a^*\) gives \(\|a^*\|\leq \|a\|\). Hence \(\|a^*\|=\|a\|\).

Self-Adjoint and Positive Elements

Order Structure

An element \(a\in A\) is self-adjoint if \(a=a^*\). In concrete operator algebras, elements of the form \(b^*b\) are interpreted as positive.

  1. Show that \(b^*b\) is self-adjoint for every \(b\in A\).
  2. For \(A=M_n(\mathbb C)\), show that \(B^*B\) is positive semidefinite in the usual matrix sense.
  3. Explain why positivity is a bridge between algebraic form and geometric measurement.
  4. Give an example of a self-adjoint matrix that is not positive semidefinite.
Hint
For matrices, compute \(\langle B^*Bv,v\rangle\). The expression should turn into a norm squared.
Solution Sketch
Since \((b^*b)^*=b^*(b^*)^*=b^*b\), the element \(b^*b\) is self-adjoint. In matrices, \(\langle B^*Bv,v\rangle=\langle Bv,Bv\rangle=\|Bv\|^2\geq 0\), so \(B^*B\) is positive semidefinite.
4

Spectrum and Spectral Radius

Spectral Thinking

For a unital algebra \(A\), the spectrum of \(a\in A\) is

\[\sigma(a)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb C: a-\lambda 1 \text{ is not invertible}\}.\]

The spectral radius is \(r(a)=\sup\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\sigma(a)\}\).

  1. Compute the spectrum of a diagonal matrix in \(M_n(\mathbb C)\).
  2. Compute the spectral radius of \(\mathrm{diag}(2,-3,i)\).
  3. Explain why spectrum is a nonlinear invariant of an algebra element.
  4. For normal matrices, compare \(r(A)\) and \(\|A\|\) under the operator norm.
Hint
A diagonal matrix is invertible exactly when none of its diagonal entries are zero.
Discussion
Spectrum is one of the deep ways C*-algebras turn algebra into geometry. An element is studied not only by its formula, but by the shape of the complex set that records its possible “observable values.”

The Commutative Model: Continuous Functions

Gelfand Picture

Let \(X\) be a compact Hausdorff space. The algebra \(C(X)\) of continuous complex-valued functions on \(X\) becomes a C*-algebra with pointwise operations, involution \(f^*(x)=\overline{f(x)}\), and norm

\[\|f\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)|.\]
  1. Verify that \(f^*f\) is the function \(x\mapsto |f(x)|^2\).
  2. Prove the C*-identity for \(C(X)\): \(\|f^*f\|_\infty=\|f\|_\infty^2\).
  3. Explain why compactness makes the supremum norm especially well-behaved.
  4. For \(X=\{1,2,3\}\), identify \(C(X)\) with a familiar finite-dimensional algebra.
Hint
The supremum of \(|f(x)|^2\) is the square of the supremum of \(|f(x)|\), because squaring is increasing on \([0,\infty)\).
Solution Sketch
Since \((f^*f)(x)=\overline{f(x)}f(x)=|f(x)|^2\), \[\|f^*f\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)|^2=\left(\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)|\right)^2=\|f\|_\infty^2.\] For a three-point space, \(C(X)\cong \mathbb C^3\) with coordinatewise operations.

Projections as Algebraic Geometry

Idempotents · Subspaces

A projection in a C*-algebra is an element \(p\in A\) satisfying

\[p=p^*=p^2.\]
  1. Show that an orthogonal projection matrix satisfies this definition.
  2. Find all projections in \(C(X)\), where \(X\) is connected.
  3. Find all projections in \(C(\{1,2,3\})\).
  4. Explain why projections behave like “noncommutative characteristic functions.”
Hint
In \(C(X)\), the equation \(p^2=p\) says \(p(x)^2=p(x)\) for each \(x\). Thus each value is either \(0\) or \(1\).
Solution Sketch
For \(C(X)\), a projection is a continuous function from \(X\) into \(\{0,1\}\). If \(X\) is connected, the only such functions are constant, so the only projections are \(0\) and \(1\). For a three-point space, projections correspond to choosing any subset of the three points, so there are \(2^3=8\) projections.

States as Noncommutative Probability Measures

Positive Linear Functionals

A state on a unital C*-algebra \(A\) is a linear functional \(\varphi:A\to\mathbb C\) such that \(\varphi(a^*a)\geq 0\) for all \(a\in A\), and \(\varphi(1)=1\).

  1. For \(A=M_n(\mathbb C)\), show that \(\varphi(A)=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(A)\) is a state.
  2. For \(A=C(X)\), explain why integration against a probability measure defines a state.
  3. Why does the condition \(\varphi(a^*a)\geq 0\) resemble positivity of expectation?
  4. Explain how this idea prepares the way for free probability.
Hint
For matrices, use \(\operatorname{Tr}(B^*B)=\sum_{i,j}|b_{ij}|^2\). For functions, use \(|f|^2\geq 0\).
Discussion
A state lets us speak probabilistically inside an algebra. In commutative algebras, this recovers ordinary probability measures. In noncommutative algebras, it becomes the conceptual gateway to quantum probability and free probability.

Mini-Synthesis: What Does the C*-Identity Do?

Conceptual Essay · Seminar Synthesis

Write a short mathematical reflection, about 500–800 words, responding to the following prompt:

The C*-identity \(\|a^*a\|=\|a\|^2\) appears at first to be a technical constraint. Explain why, after this problem set, it should instead be regarded as the structural bridge between algebra, geometry, order, spectrum, and probability.
  1. Use at least two examples from this problem set.
  2. Discuss both matrix algebras and commutative function algebras.
  3. Include a comment on positivity or states.
  4. Conclude by explaining why C*-algebras are natural homes for noncommutative mathematics.
Possible Structure
Begin with matrices as the concrete model. Then shift to \(C(X)\) as the commutative model. Use the C*-identity to connect norm and adjoint. Then discuss positivity through \(a^*a\), projections as generalized characteristic functions, and states as generalized probability measures.

Closing Note

This first unit is meant to feel like the opening movement of a seminar: the student learns the grammar, but also begins to see why the grammar is unusually rigid and expressive. In later units, this structure can be developed toward representation theory, spectral theory, compact operators, von Neumann algebras, free probability, and computational operator algebra experiments.
