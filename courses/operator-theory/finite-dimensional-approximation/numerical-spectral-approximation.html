Finite Dimensional Approximation

Numerical Spectral Approximation

A self-contained worksheet on the numerical range, spectral geometry, and the Toeplitz–Hausdorff theorem on convexity.

Guiding question

How much of an operator can be seen through quadratic measurement?

Let \(A\in M_n(\mathbb C)\). The eigenvalues of \(A\) tell us where \(A-\lambda I\) fails to be invertible. But there is another spectral object, more geometric and more stable under numerical approximation: the numerical range.

Definition. The numerical range of \(A\) is \[ W(A)=\{ \langle Ax,x\rangle : x\in \mathbb C^n,\ \|x\|=1\}. \]

This set records all possible expectation values of the operator \(A\) against unit vectors. It is a bridge between linear algebra, spectral theory, quantum measurement, and numerical approximation.

Part I. First Examples

Problem 1. Suppose \(A=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)\). Show that \[ \langle Ax,x\rangle = \sum_{j=1}^n \lambda_j |x_j|^2. \] Explain why \(W(A)\) is contained in the convex hull of \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n\).
Problem 2. If \(A=A^*\), prove that \(W(A)\subset \mathbb R\). Then show that \(W(A)\) is the interval \[ [\lambda_{\min}(A),\lambda_{\max}(A)]. \]
Problem 3. Let \[ A= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \] Compute \(\langle Ax,x\rangle\) for \(x=(\cos t,e^{i\theta}\sin t)\). Show that \(W(A)\) is the closed disk centered at \(0\) with radius \(1/2\).

Part II. The Two-Dimensional Case

The heart of the Toeplitz–Hausdorff theorem is already visible in dimension two.

Lemma. If \(B\in M_2(\mathbb C)\), then \(W(B)\) is an elliptical disk, possibly degenerate. In particular, \(W(B)\) is convex.

To see why, put \(B\) into Schur triangular form: \[ B= \begin{pmatrix} \alpha & c\\ 0 & \beta \end{pmatrix}. \] For a unit vector \(x=(\cos t,e^{i\theta}\sin t)\), one obtains \[ \langle Bx,x\rangle = \alpha\cos^2 t+\beta\sin^2 t + c e^{i\theta}\sin t\cos t. \]

Problem 4. For fixed \(t\), explain why the last expression traces a circle as \(\theta\) varies.
Problem 5. Explain geometrically why allowing \(t\) to vary fills an elliptical disk with foci \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\).

Part III. The Toeplitz–Hausdorff Theorem

Toeplitz–Hausdorff Theorem. For every matrix \(A\in M_n(\mathbb C)\), the numerical range \[ W(A)=\{\langle Ax,x\rangle:\|x\|=1\} \] is a convex subset of \(\mathbb C\).

Proof.

Let \(z,w\in W(A)\). Then there exist unit vectors \(x,y\in\mathbb C^n\) such that \[ z=\langle Ax,x\rangle, \qquad w=\langle Ay,y\rangle. \]

Let \(M=\operatorname{span}\{x,y\}\). This subspace has dimension at most two. Let \(P_M\) denote the orthogonal projection onto \(M\), and consider the compression \[ B=P_M A|_M. \]

For every unit vector \(u\in M\), \[ \langle Bu,u\rangle = \langle P_MAu,u\rangle = \langle Au,u\rangle. \] Therefore \[ W(B)\subseteq W(A). \]

Since \(x,y\in M\), both \(z\) and \(w\) belong to \(W(B)\). But \(B\) acts on a space of dimension at most two, so by the two-dimensional lemma, \(W(B)\) is convex.

Hence the entire line segment \[ tz+(1-t)w,\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1, \] lies in \(W(B)\), and therefore lies in \(W(A)\).

Since any two points of \(W(A)\) are joined by a line segment contained in \(W(A)\), the numerical range \(W(A)\) is convex. \(\square\)

Why This Belongs Here

The numerical range is especially important for finite-dimensional approximation because it behaves more continuously and geometrically than the raw spectrum alone. In computation, spectra may be unstable under perturbation, while numerical ranges and pseudospectral regions often reveal the operational geometry more faithfully.

The Toeplitz–Hausdorff theorem is therefore not merely a classical result. It says that the expectation-value shadow of an operator is always convex: a hidden geometric regularity inside arbitrary complex matrices.
